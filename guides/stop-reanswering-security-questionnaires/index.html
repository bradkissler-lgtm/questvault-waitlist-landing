How to stop re-answering security questionnaires every sales cycle

Most early B2B SaaS teams do not have a questionnaire problem. They have a reuse problem: the answers already exist, they are scattered, and nobody wants to be the person who ships an unverified claim to a buyer’s security team.

Why deals stall

Enterprise buyers treat vendor security review as a gate, not a formality. Procurement sends a workbook or a portal. Sales forwards it to whoever last touched a similar file. That person hunts Slack, Drive, and old XLSX exports. The clock starts on a date that was already tight.

The stall is rarely “we have no security program.” It is operational:

  • Search time. The same encryption, access-control, and subprocessors questions arrive in new wording. Finding last quarter’s answer takes longer than editing it.
  • Review fear. Founders and security owners know a copied sentence can be wrong after a vendor change, a new region, or an expired control. They slow the send rather than risk a customer-facing error.
  • Format tax. Buyers want their spreadsheet, with their IDs, tabs, and drop-downs intact. Reformatting a SIG- or CAIQ-style workbook into a nicer internal doc creates a second project.
  • Owner vacuum. On a five-person team, CS, the founder, and whoever holds the latest audit PDF all think someone else is “the questionnaire person.”

None of that is solved by a longer all-hands. It is solved by a library of reviewed answers, a mapping step onto the buyer’s file, and a named reviewer before export. If you sell cloud software, you will also see SIG and CAIQ workbooks; they are different questionnaires from different publishers, and they still land as spreadsheets.

The spreadsheet method

The method is unglamorous and it works. You keep one internal library. You never permanently replace the buyer’s workbook with your own layout.

1. Capture finished work once

Export or copy every questionnaire you have already sent: custom VSAs, SIG-style workbooks, CAIQ spreadsheets, and one-off Excel files from procurement. Store the original files for evidence. Pull the Q&A pairs into a single library sheet or database. Do not treat a folder of “final_v7.xlsx” as the library.

2. Normalize the answer, not the buyer’s question

Each library row is your control statement, not a clone of one customer’s prompt. When a new file arrives, you match their question to your row, then paste or map into their cell. That is how you stop rewriting “Describe encryption at rest” twelve different ways.

3. Keep the fields that make reuse defensible

A reusable row is more than a paragraph. At minimum track: canonical answer, domain or topic, evidence pointer (policy name, report section, ticket, or diagram—not a vague “we’re compliant”), owner, last-verified date, status (draft / reviewed / exception), and customer-specific notes stored separately so they do not leak into the next deal.

4. Fill the original workbook

Work in the file you were sent. Preserve question IDs, tab names, data-validation lists, and any “not applicable” logic. Buyers review in their template. A rebuilt PDF slows them down and looks like you did not follow instructions. Shared Assessments’ SIG materials describe SIG as a scoped questionnaire that commonly arrives as a structured workbook; CSA describes CAIQ as a downloadable spreadsheet of yes/no questions tied to the Cloud Controls Matrix. In both cases the delivery format is the point.

5. Review, then send

Suggested fills are a head start. They are not a send. A human checks scope, dates, shared-responsibility language, and exceptions. If you cannot cite evidence, leave the cell blank or mark follow-up rather than inventing a control.

What this is not. The spreadsheet method is not a GRC program, a certification, or permission to reuse last year’s language without checking whether the control still exists. Official SIG content is licensed by Shared Assessments; unofficial “free SIG downloads” are a legal and accuracy risk. Obtain the file from the buyer or the publisher.

For the library shape itself, see how to run a SaaS answer bank.

Build a DIY answer bank

You can run the library in Sheets or Excel for a long time. The failure mode is not the tool. It is missing owners and stale dates.

  1. One canonical row per control topic. Merge duplicates. Conflicting answers are worse than a gap.
  2. Evidence is a pointer, not a vibe. “Yes, encryption at rest, see Infrastructure policy §3, reviewed March 2026” survives the next cycle. “Yes” does not.
  3. Exceptions live in their own field. Compensating controls and roadmaps expire. Date them.
  4. Assign an owner and a reviewer. On a small team that can be the same person on Tuesday and a founder on Friday—as long as the name is on the row.
  5. Re-verify on a calendar, not on panic. Quarterly is enough for many early SaaS teams. After a material change (new subprocessors, new region, new auth model), re-verify the affected rows immediately.
  6. Map inbound files to the library. Do not start a new essay in the customer workbook unless the question is actually new.

DIY breaks down when two people edit copies of the library, when matching questions by memory, or when the inbound volume outruns the review calendar. That is the point where a dedicated answer-bank workflow earns its keep—not because spreadsheets are “bad,” but because versioning and suggested matching become the bottleneck.

When to buy a filler

Buy software when the spreadsheet method is working and still hurting. Skip the purchase if you do not yet have answers worth reusing.

A filler is worth paying for when several of these are true:

  • You complete questionnaires every month, not twice a year.
  • More than one person needs the same library, with status and review ownership.
  • Inbound files are XLSX/CSV and you want suggested fills against your own past Q&A—not a generic LLM paragraph.
  • You will still review before send. If a vendor claims you can skip human review on security claims, that is a process risk, not a feature.

Buy a broader platform when the job has changed:

  • Buyers insist on web portals (OneTrust-style, service-now-style, or vendor risk portals) and you need a browser extension or managed portal filling. A spreadsheet exporter will lose that comparison. Conveyor’s public pricing page lists a browser extension for customer portals on paid questionnaire automation; HyperComply’s AWS listing emphasizes Excel/Word import plus optional full-service handling.
  • You want a trust center / trust page as the primary motion so some customers self-serve documents instead of sending a workbook.
  • You want a managed analyst to import, fill, and grammar-check under an SLA, and you can pay Full-Service bands starting at $10,000/year on the public HyperComply AWS table.

For early B2B SaaS, the usual sequence is: DIY library → self-serve answer bank → enterprise automation. Jumping to the $9,600+/year band because the last deal was painful is how teams buy a trust-center suite they do not operate.
